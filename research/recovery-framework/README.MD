# Recovery Framework

## Purpose

The Recovery Framework provides a conceptual foundation for understanding how people preserve or regain agency while living with chronic illness, repeated trauma, and other high-stress environments.

This framework investigates recovery as an adaptive process occurring across individuals, relationships, institutions, and technology. It asks how people interpret signals, coordinate support, navigate complex systems, and make meaningful decisions under persistent uncertainty.

The framework integrates established research across neuroscience, psychology, behavioral medicine, health services research, and systems science while proposing new concepts intended for empirical investigation through participatory research.

Core Definitions
Patient Agency
Patient agency is the dynamic capacity to interpret health-related information, make informed and values-aligned decisions, mobilize personal and community resources, navigate institutional systems, and meaningfully influence one's health and recovery despite uncertainty or systemic constraints.
Agency is not independence.
Agency exists within environmental, social, financial, institutional, and physiological constraints. A person's capacity to act is shaped not only by individual knowledge and motivation, but also by whether information is understandable, support is accessible, and institutions are navigable.
This definition builds upon complementary concepts including self-efficacy, patient activation, patient empowerment, shared decision making, self-determination theory, health literacy, and psychological flexibility.
Recovery Capacity
Recovery capacity is a person's changing ability to interpret internal and external signals, access appropriate support, respond adaptively to stress, and preserve or regain agency over time.
Recovery capacity is intentionally broader than symptom management.
People may experience persistent pain, disability, or psychological distress while simultaneously increasing their ability to navigate healthcare, coordinate support, adapt to changing circumstances, and participate meaningfully in their own recovery.

Likewise, symptom improvement alone may not represent durable recovery if agency has not increased.
Foundational Assumption
Many populations served by MCCR experience repeated uncertainty, adversity, institutional complexity, or prolonged stress.

Across chronic illness, trauma, substance recovery, disability, and other high-stress circumstances, recovery often requires interpreting signals originating from multiple interacting systems simultaneously. These include physiological processes, cognition, emotion, relationships, community resources, healthcare systems, employment, housing, and public institutions.
This framework investigates whether common mechanisms govern recovery across these seemingly distinct conditions.
Rather than organizing recovery exclusively around diagnoses, the framework examines how distress is interpreted, distributed, regulated, and supported across a person's environment.

### Source Displacement

One proposed mechanism within this framework is Source Displacement, or Signal-Source Misattribution.
Source displacement refers to a state in which distress generated or amplified within one physiological, psychological, relational, or institutional domain becomes interpreted, expressed, or acted upon primarily within another domain.

Examples may include:

- institutional stress experienced primarily as escalation of physical symptoms
- physical pain interpreted primarily as interpersonal rejection
- emotional threat expressed through conflict with a safer relationship
- uncertainty managed through compulsive behavior or substance use
- diffuse distress becoming attached to a single highly salient explanation

The framework does not assume these examples are pathological. Instead, they are treated as adaptive responses that may emerge under persistent uncertainty or constrained environments.

The central research question becomes:
Under what conditions does distress originating in one domain become interpreted primarily through another?

Potential mechanisms include:

- ambiguous signal origin
- attentional capture
- prior expectations
- learned associations
- perceived safety of expression
- power asymmetry
- avoidance of less tolerable explanations
- limited access to interpretive language
- limited access to trusted support
- immediate reinforcement

These mechanisms represent hypotheses for empirical investigation rather than established conclusions.

### Recovery as a Distributed Process

Recovery is often evaluated at the level of the individual.

This framework proposes that recovery may instead emerge from interactions between individuals and the systems surrounding them.

These systems include relationships, families, peers, clinicians, community organizations, employers, insurers, digital technologies, and public institutions.

Recovery capacity therefore depends not only on individual behavior but also on how regulation, information, resources, and decision making are distributed across those systems.

This perspective shifts recovery from being viewed solely as an individual responsibility toward understanding recovery as a property of people interacting with supportive or unsupportive networks.

## Operationalizing Emerging Concepts

### Co-regulation Networks

Co-regulation networks describe how emotional, informational, practical, and physiological regulation is distributed across relationships and institutions.
Potential indicators include:

Network Structure

- number of trusted relationships
- diversity of supporters
- network density
- centralization
- redundancy
- frequency of interaction

Network Function

- emotional regulation
- informational support
- practical assistance
- healthcare coordination
- institutional advocacy
- reciprocity
- perceived reliability

### Institutional Friction

Institutional friction describes the cumulative effort required to successfully navigate systems that influence recovery.
Examples include healthcare, insurance, housing, employment, education, disability services, transportation, and legal systems.

Potential indicators include:
- administrative burden
- procedural complexity
- documentation requirements
- readability of information
- accessibility of patient rights
- wait times
- repeated requests
- conflicting guidance
- number of interactions required before receiving support


### Recovery Capacity

Recovery capacity is expected to be measurable across multiple interacting dimensions rather than a single score.

Potential dimensions include:
- signal interpretation
- adaptive flexibility
- agency
- resource accessibility
- institutional navigation
- resilience following setbacks
- co-regulation
- continuity of supportive relationships

Future work will investigate reliable operational definitions and participatory validation of these constructs with communities of lived experience.

## Initial Research Questions

- The framework currently investigates questions including:
- Under what conditions does distress originating in one domain become attributed to another?
-What information can be made directly interpretable to patients and communities without replacing clinical expertise?
- What assumptions do existing digital therapeutics make about recovery?
- Does improving symptom management without improving patient agency produce durable recovery?
- How can co-regulation networks be measured?
- How can institutional friction be quantified?
- Can recovery be understood as a property of a person and their surrounding network rather than the individual alone?
